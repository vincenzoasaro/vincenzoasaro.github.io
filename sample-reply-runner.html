Reply Runner: practising empathy at running speed

A five-minute game that teaches new support advisors to write replies that sound human, by making them choose under gentle pressure and explaining every choice.

Audience
New customer-support advisors
Format
Browser game, desktop and mobile
Length
About five minutes, 15 scenarios
Approach
Scenario-based practice with feedback

Play it here. Turn on relaxed pace on the start screen if you'd like to read without a timer.

The learning problem

Tone is the part of support writing that new advisors find hardest to learn from a style guide. Most of them already know they should be polite. What they struggle with is the gap between polite and helpful: the scripted apology that sounds dismissive, the confident promise that later breaks, the formal reply to a customer who wrote casually.

That's a judgment skill, and judgment is built by making decisions and seeing their consequences. So instead of explaining good tone, the game asks learners to pick between three plausible replies, again and again, with feedback on every choice.

The company, customers and messages are invented, so the game can be shared publicly. They're modelled on the situations advisors meet in their first weeks on a support desk.

Design decisions

Objectives drive the content

Six skills are stated before play, and every scenario is written to practise exactly one. The runner mechanic exists only to turn "which reply would you send?" into a quick, repeatable decision.

Wrong answers are realistic

Each wrong lane is a mistake real advisors make. Learners practise telling good from almost-good, which is where the real difficulty lies, not good from absurd.

Feedback explains, every time

A miss stops the run, explains why the reply falls short, then shows a stronger one and why it works. A hit gets a one-line reason, so even a lucky guess teaches something.

Difficulty rises with competence

Level 1 contrasts warm and cold. Level 2 hides the traps inside polite language and adds tone matching. Level 3 brings pressure, boundaries and a safety issue, with less reading time.

Stakes that don't lock people out

Customer trust adds tension, but right answers win it back and each level starts fresh. Mistakes cost something without blocking anyone from later content.

Learners control the pace

Relaxed pace removes the timer for slower readers and second-language speakers. Sending early earns a bonus, so confident learners can move fast without forcing everyone to.

The debrief points to what's next

Results are broken down by skill, with a short tip for each weak one. "Practise the ones I missed" replays only those scenarios, with the replies reshuffled, for retrieval practice.

The six skills, and the traps that test them

Each skill is paired with a wrong answer that sounds reasonable at first glance.

SkillA trap the game usesWhy it misses
Acknowledge feelingsI apologise for any inconvenience this may have caused.A scripted line. "Any" and "may have" shrink a real problem.
Own the issueOur drivers work for a separate company.The customer chose your company, so the complaint is yours to handle.
Clear, positive languageUnfortunately, that is against our policy.A wall instead of a reason and an alternative.
Match the toneDear Customer, we acknowledge receipt of your request.Robotic in reply to a relaxed one-line chat.
Set clear expectationsAbsolutely, 100% guaranteed!Reassuring now, but it promises something the courier controls.
Say no kindlyFine, I'll give you a year for free.Gives in to pressure and teaches that threats work.

Taking it into a real program

As a portfolio piece, the game stands alone. In a live onboarding program, I would add:

  • Scenarios from real tickets. Anonymized examples, reviewed with subject-matter experts, so the language matches the product and the customers.
  • Tracking. Results sent to the learning platform for each skill, so trainers can see where a cohort struggles and adjust live sessions.
  • A link to performance. A pilot with one cohort, comparing quality-review scores on tone with a group trained the usual way.
  • Spaced follow-ups. Short practice rounds a week and a month later, drawn from each learner's missed scenarios.

Designed and built in plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript, with no plug-ins or tracking. All companies, customers and messages are fictional.
